## Grammar: Numbers from 11 to 19

<https://learngerman.dw.com/en/numbers-from-11-to-19/l-37265621/gr-38307006>

The numbers *11* and *12* are irregular.

From *13,* the formation of the numbers is mostly regular. In German, the order of numbers is opposite to that in English. First comes the single digit (e.g. drei, vier, fünf...) and then the ten (*zehn*):

 

The numbers *16* and *17* are slightly irregular. The single digits (*sechs, sieben*) lose their endings.

|     |                   |              |
|-----|-------------------|--------------|
| 11  |                   | **elf**      |
| 12  |                   | **zwölf**    |
| 13  | drei + zehn       | dreizehn     |
| 14  | vier + zehn       | vierzehn     |
| 15  | fünf + zehn       | fünfzehn     |
| 16  | **sechs** + zehn  | **sech**zehn |
| 17  | **sieben** + zehn | **sieb**zehn |
| 18  | acht + zehn       | achtzehn     |
| 19  | neun + zehn       | neunzehn     |

## Grammar: Numbers from 20 to 100

<https://learngerman.dw.com/en/numbers-from-20-to-100/l-37265621/gr-38307981>

With numbers over *20,* the single digit comes first, and then the multiple of ten. There are two special features to note:

- The number *eins* loses the *-s *when it is combined with a ten.
- There is an *und* between the single digit and the multiple of ten.

|     |                  |
|-----|------------------|
| 21  | einundzwanzig    |
| 22  | zweiundzwanzig   |
| 23  | dreiundzwanzig   |
| 24  | vierundzwanzig   |
| 25  | fünfundzwanzig   |
| 26  | sechsundzwanzig  |
| 27  | siebenundzwanzig |
| 28  | achtundzwanzig   |
| 29  | neunundzwanzig   |
| 30  | dreißig          |

 

The full multiple of ten is usually formed from the single digit + *-zig.* But there are some exceptions: *10*, *20*, *30, 60* and *70* have an irregular form.

|     |                                    |
|-----|------------------------------------|
| 10  | **zehn**                           |
| 20  | **zwanzig**                        |
| 30  | **dreißig**                        |
| 40  | vierzig                            |
| 50  | fünfzig                            |
| 60  | **sechzig**                        |
| 70  | **siebzig**                        |
| 80  | achtzig                            |
| 90  | neunzig                            |
| 100 | einhundert (short form: *hundert*) |

 

You can form all the numbers from *21* to *99* from the following elements:

## Culture: Celebrating birthdays in the office

<https://learngerman.dw.com/en/celebrating-birthdays-in-the-office/l-37265621/rs-40966362>

Birthdays at a workplace - Every company has its own rules, depending on the company's size. It's best to ask around to see how birthdays are celebrated in the company. Sometimes the birthdays of individual employees within a department or work group are available in a public calendar. You can wish your colleagues a happy birthday. They often bring a treat for everyone, like a cake, which is eaten together during a break.

## Culture: Happy Birthday!

<https://learngerman.dw.com/en/happy-birthday/l-37265621/rs-39370325>

Birthdays are important in Germany. Adults as well as children celebrate their birthday with family and friends. The person celebrating his or her birthday receives flowers, a cake with candles, and gifts.

If it's your friend's birthday, you should wish them a "Happy Birthday!". You buy a present only for good friends, or if you've been invited to a party. If you don't know what you should get the person, it's okay to ask them if they have a particular wish. Many Germans use the English expression "Happy Birthday!" rather than the German "*Herzlichen Glückwunsch zum Geburtstag*".
